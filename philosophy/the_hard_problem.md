What is subjectivity? And why is it that it contradicts with the objective world championed by the scientific and mathematical methods? How do we reconcile this fundamental contradiction? To answer these questions, we need to understand the phenomenology of subjectivity, and provide a precise and reasonable definition. For one thing, there is an inherent uniqueness in our experience with our own subjectivity. While the intuition is clear that being you is a phenomenon which is not replicable, it is hard, if not impossible, to extract precision out of this intuition. It is easy to conclude that such property is secondary in the generating process of consciousness. However, it is also the same intuition that underlies the so-called hard problem of consciousness – that simply the existing description does not align with our experience of consciousness at a fundamental level. 

One useful way to interpret the relationship between subjective experiences, and one objective observable facts, is whether a first-handed experience, in contrast to second-handed experience, exists. A second-handed experience is a set of known information that can be known earlier despite of currently being displayed. A novel, a play, a movie, or a concert – they are all second-handed experience, because they can be read, performed, displayed, or rehearsed earlier. The first-hand experience, however, is not there for display. The same experience cannot be experienced twice; neither can one experience at two places at once. Although the sensory input information may be replicable, being consciousness of those information has an aspect that is fundamentally irreversible and irreplicable – corresponding to the uniqueness in the time and space domain. It is first-handed because it can be aware of information despite of the context of the information, and it is first-handed because it can be known to no others. 

Irreversibility, irreplicability, context independence, and uniqueness allows one to be aware of something before a description is made to the process of being aware – such that the experience is first-handed instead of second-handed. These apparent properties contrast facts that are perfected by the scientific method, which emphasises on the opposite: reproducibility in space and time. A scientific fact can be replicated across multiple labs, and can be repeated many times. Principle of physics are universal, but are always framed as a local statement targetting specific mathematical or physical set-up. For example, although the equivalent principle universal, it a statement on the local reference frame where laws of physics are observed. Consequently, they are by no means unique, because one can set up these facts anywhere given the right techniques. 

Such apparent incompatibility between our personal experience, and the objective reality, is what constitutes contradictions between subjectivity and objectivity. 

This contradiction is pointed out in the 48th chapter of the 道德經 (Tao Te Ching): 

為學日益，為道日損，

The pursuit of learning is gaining, and the pursuit of Tao is losing. 

with a further interesting elaboration:

損之又損，以至於無為，無為而無不為。

Losing after losing, until the arrival to the lack of pursuit. Through the lack of pursuit, there is nothing one does not pursue. 

The typical interpretation one can find in the internet will connect this excerpt with moral and political philosophy. However, in authentic Taoism, the pursuit of learning is really about the pursuit of knowledge, and the pursuit of Tao is about the pursuit of internal realisation -- the way of the natural (道法自然). If objective knowledge can be formalized as reproducibility and replicability, then the pursuit of Tao, and therefore subjective inquiry, requires the letting go of reproducibility and replicability, in order to approach the natural way -- which emphasizes addressing questions like why you, and you in particular, is born in this world through direct experience rather than seeking knowledges. 

Isn't this merely an example of Taoism favouring the ontological legitimacy of subjectivity, facing the hard-problem of consciousness? Although the author does not intend to provide the answer immediately to how Eastern philosophy deals with this situation, the answer is actually hidden in the elaboration.
